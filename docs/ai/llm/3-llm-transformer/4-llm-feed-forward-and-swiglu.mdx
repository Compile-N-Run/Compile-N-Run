# Feed-Forward Networks, GELU and SwiGLU

## Introduction

The feed-forward network (FFN, or MLP) is the least glamorous part of a Transformer block and the largest. In Llama 3.1 8B it holds 81% of each block's weights and does about the same share of the arithmetic at short context. When people say a model "stores knowledge in its weights", most of those weights sit in FFNs.

The FFN has also changed more than any other sublayer since 2017. The original used ReLU with a hidden layer four times the model width. BERT and GPT-2 switched to GELU. Since PaLM (2022) and Llama (2023), almost every LLM uses a gated variant called SwiGLU, with a hidden width that looks oddly specific: 11,008 in Llama 2 7B, 14,336 in Llama 3 8B. This page explains each step and where those numbers come from.

You will learn to:

- write the classic FFN and the SwiGLU FFN as equations and as PyTorch modules, with shapes;
- compare ReLU, GELU and SiLU numerically and explain why smooth activations are preferred;
- explain gating and read the results of Shazeer's 2020 "GLU Variants Improve Transformer";
- derive the 2/3 factor that keeps SwiGLU's parameter count equal to a 4x FFN, and reproduce Meta's hidden-size rounding rule;
- run a small matched-parameter experiment comparing GELU and SwiGLU on a CPU.

## The Position-Wise Feed-Forward Network

The 2017 Transformer's FFN is two linear maps with a nonlinearity between them, applied to each position independently:

$$
\mathrm{FFN}(x) = \phi(x W_1 + b_1)\, W_2 + b_2, \qquad W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_\mathrm{ff}}, \quad W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\mathrm{ff} \times d}
$$

Here $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is one token's (normalized) residual stream vector and $\phi$ is the activation function. The original used $\phi = \mathrm{ReLU}$ and $d_\mathrm{ff} = 4d$ (2048 for $d = 512$). GPT-2, BERT and GPT-3 kept the factor 4. Modern LLMs drop the biases.

Two facts frame everything that follows.

First, the FFN is where per-token nonlinear computation happens. Attention mixes information across positions but, apart from its softmax, is linear in the values it mixes. Without the FFN's nonlinearity, a stack of attention layers would be far less expressive. The FFN's expansion to $4d$ followed by projection back to $d$ gives each token a wide hidden layer in which to compute features.

Second, the FFN is a big lookup table. Each hidden unit $i$ has an input vector (column $i$ of $W_1$) and an output vector (row $i$ of $W_2$). The unit's activation is $\phi(x \cdot w_{1,i})$, a measure of how well the token's state matches a pattern; its contribution to the output is that activation times $w_{2,i}$. Geva et al. (2021) described FFN layers as key-value memories in exactly this sense, and showed that many keys respond to recognizable input patterns while values promote specific next tokens.

Parameter count without biases: $2 d\, d_\mathrm{ff} = 8 d^2$ for $d_\mathrm{ff} = 4d$. Forward FLOPs per token: $2 \cdot 8 d^2 = 16 d^2$, since each weight participates in one multiply and one add.

## Activation Functions: ReLU, GELU and SiLU

The activation decides how hidden units respond near zero. Three matter for LLMs.

**ReLU**, $\max(0, z)$, is cheap and was the default for a decade. Its gradient is exactly zero for negative inputs, so a unit whose pre-activation stays negative for every input stops learning ("dead" units), and its kink at zero gives a discontinuous derivative.

**GELU** (Hendrycks and Gimpel, "Gaussian Error Linear Units", 2016) weights the input by the probability that a standard normal variable is below it:

$$
\mathrm{GELU}(z) = z\, \Phi(z) = \frac{z}{2}\left(1 + \mathrm{erf}\!\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right) \approx \frac{z}{2}\left(1 + \tanh\!\left(\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\big(z + 0.044715\, z^3\big)\right)\right)
$$

It is smooth, passes small negative values (with a minimum of about $-0.17$ near $z = -0.75$), and approaches ReLU for large $\lvert z \rvert$. BERT and GPT-2 used it; GPT-2 used the tanh approximation, which survives as `gelu_pytorch_tanh` in Gemma's config.json.

**SiLU**, also called Swish, is $z\, \sigma(z)$ where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. Elfwing et al. (2017) proposed it as the sigmoid-weighted linear unit, and Ramachandran et al. (2017) found it through an automated search over activation functions and named it Swish (with an optional slope $\beta$ inside the sigmoid; $\beta = 1$ gives SiLU). Its shape is close to GELU's with a deeper negative dip (minimum about $-0.28$). In config.json it appears as `"hidden_act": "silu"`.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

x = torch.tensor([-3.0, -1.0, -0.5, 0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 3.0])
print("x        ", x.tolist())
print("ReLU     ", [round(v, 4) for v in F.relu(x).tolist()])
print("GELU     ", [round(v, 4) for v in F.gelu(x).tolist()])                      # exact, uses erf
print("GELU tanh", [round(v, 4) for v in F.gelu(x, approximate="tanh").tolist()])  # GPT-2 / Gemma
print("SiLU     ", [round(v, 4) for v in F.silu(x).tolist()])                      # x * sigmoid(x)
```

```text
x         [-3.0, -1.0, -0.5, 0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 3.0]
ReLU      [0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 3.0]
GELU      [-0.0041, -0.1587, -0.1543, 0.0, 0.3457, 0.8413, 2.9959]
GELU tanh [-0.0036, -0.1588, -0.1543, 0.0, 0.3457, 0.8412, 2.9964]
SiLU      [-0.1423, -0.2689, -0.1888, 0.0, 0.3112, 0.7311, 2.8577]
```

You can check one value by hand: $\mathrm{GELU}(1) = 1 \cdot \Phi(1) = 0.8413$, the familiar 84th percentile of the normal distribution, and $\mathrm{SiLU}(1) = 1/(1 + e^{-1}) = 0.7311$. The tanh approximation differs from exact GELU in the fourth decimal, which is why you should match whichever one a checkpoint was trained with.

On their own, these activations make modest differences. In Shazeer's T5 experiments (below), replacing ReLU with GELU or Swish in a standard FFN changed held-out log-perplexity by less than 0.02. The large gain came from gating.

## Gated Linear Units and SwiGLU

A gated linear unit (Dauphin et al., "Language Modeling with Gated Convolutional Networks", 2016) multiplies two linear projections of the same input elementwise, one of them passed through an activation:

$$
\mathrm{GLU}(x) = \sigma(x W) \odot (x V)
$$

The activated branch acts as a gate: for each hidden unit it decides, per token, how much of the other branch's value passes through. Shazeer (2020) replaced the sigmoid with other activations and put the result in place of the FFN's first layer:

$$
\mathrm{FFN}_\mathrm{SwiGLU}(x) = \big(\mathrm{SiLU}(x W) \odot x V\big)\, W_2, \qquad
\mathrm{FFN}_\mathrm{GEGLU}(x) = \big(\mathrm{GELU}(x W) \odot x V\big)\, W_2
$$

with $W, V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times h}$ and $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d}$, all without biases. In Hugging Face code these three matrices are named `gate_proj` ($W$), `up_proj` ($V$) and `down_proj` ($W_2$), and the forward pass is literally `down_proj(act_fn(gate_proj(x)) * up_proj(x))`.

Why might gating help? A plain FFN unit can only output $\phi(\text{one linear score})$. A gated unit outputs the product of two linear functions of the input, a quadratic interaction, so each unit can express "feature A, but only when feature B is present". The gate is also smooth and input-dependent rather than a fixed threshold. Shazeer's paper offers no theory; its conclusion famously attributes the success "to divine benevolence". The evidence is empirical.

Shazeer trained T5-base-sized encoder-decoders (12 + 12 layers, $d = 768$) on C4 span corruption, with all variants matched in parameters and compute. Held-out log-perplexity (lower is better):

| FFN variant | 65,536 steps | 524,288 steps |
|---|---|---|
| ReLU (baseline) | 1.997 | 1.677 |
| GELU | 1.983 | 1.679 |
| Swish | 1.994 | 1.683 |
| GLU (sigmoid gate) | 1.982 | 1.663 |
| Bilinear (no activation) | 1.960 | 1.648 |
| ReGLU | 1.953 | 1.645 |
| GEGLU | 1.942 | 1.633 |
| SwiGLU | 1.944 | 1.636 |

Two observations. GEGLU and SwiGLU are tied for best, about 0.04 nats better than the baseline, which is a large gain for a change of a few lines. And even the bilinear layer, with no activation at all, beats ReLU; the multiplicative interaction matters more than which activation shapes the gate. The gated variants also scored best on most GLUE and SuperGLUE fine-tuning tasks.

PaLM (2022) adopted SwiGLU, citing this paper, and Llama (2023) followed. Gemma uses the GEGLU variant (`hidden_activation: gelu_pytorch_tanh` with a gate). gpt-oss uses a modified SwiGLU that clamps the gate and value branches to at most 7 and uses $z\,\sigma(1.702 z)$ as the activation, adding 1 to the value branch.

## Why the Hidden Size Is 8/3 of the Width

SwiGLU has three weight matrices instead of two. To compare fairly with a $4d$ FFN, Shazeer shrank the hidden size so the parameter count stays the same:

$$
\underbrace{2 \cdot d \cdot 4d}_{\text{classic FFN}} = 8 d^2 = \underbrace{3 \cdot d \cdot h}_{\text{SwiGLU}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = \frac{8}{3} d \approx 2.67\, d
$$

In his T5 experiments, $d_\mathrm{ff} = 3072$ became $h = 2048$, exactly $2/3$ of it. FLOPs scale the same way, since each weight is used once per token.

Llama's reference code (`model.py` in Meta's repositories) computes the hidden size from a nominal `hidden_dim = 4 * dim`, then takes 2/3 of it, optionally multiplies by `ffn_dim_multiplier`, and rounds up to a multiple of `multiple_of`:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)

class GeluMLP(nn.Module):                      # GPT-2 style: two matrices
    def __init__(self, d, hidden):
        super().__init__()
        self.up = nn.Linear(d, hidden, bias=False)
        self.down = nn.Linear(hidden, d, bias=False)

    def forward(self, x):                      # (B, T, d)
        return self.down(F.gelu(self.up(x)))   # (B, T, hidden) -> (B, T, d)

class SwiGLU(nn.Module):                       # Llama style: three matrices
    def __init__(self, d, hidden):
        super().__init__()
        self.gate = nn.Linear(d, hidden, bias=False)   # W
        self.up = nn.Linear(d, hidden, bias=False)     # V
        self.down = nn.Linear(hidden, d, bias=False)   # W_2

    def forward(self, x):                              # (B, T, d)
        return self.down(F.silu(self.gate(x)) * self.up(x))

def llama_hidden(d, multiple_of, ffn_dim_multiplier=None):
    """Hidden size rule from Meta's reference model.py."""
    hidden = int(2 * (4 * d) / 3)
    if ffn_dim_multiplier is not None:
        hidden = int(ffn_dim_multiplier * hidden)
    return multiple_of * ((hidden + multiple_of - 1) // multiple_of)

d = 768
count = lambda m: sum(p.numel() for p in m.parameters())
print("GELU MLP, hidden 4d   :", count(GeluMLP(d, 4 * d)))
print("SwiGLU,   hidden 8d/3 :", count(SwiGLU(d, 8 * d // 3)))

x = torch.randn(2, 5, d)
print("output shape:", tuple(SwiGLU(d, 2048)(x).shape))

print("Llama 2 7B  hidden:", llama_hidden(4096, 256))
print("Llama 3 8B  hidden:", llama_hidden(4096, 1024, 1.3))
```

```text
GELU MLP, hidden 4d   : 4718592
SwiGLU,   hidden 8d/3 : 4718592
output shape: (2, 5, 768)
Llama 2 7B  hidden: 11008
Llama 3 8B  hidden: 14336
```

Follow the Llama 2 7B case by hand: $4 \times 4096 = 16384$; two thirds of that, truncated, is 10,922; rounded up to a multiple of 256 it becomes $43 \times 256 = 11008$. That is the `intermediate_size` in Llama 2 7B's config.json, a ratio of 2.6875.

For Llama 3.1 8B, the original `params.json` has `"ffn_dim_multiplier": 1.3` and `"multiple_of": 1024`. So $10922 \times 1.3 = 14198.6$, truncated to 14,198, rounded up to $14 \times 1024 = 14336$: a ratio of exactly 3.5. Meta chose to spend more parameters on the FFN than parameter matching would suggest.

The rounding is for hardware. GPU matrix-multiply units and tensor-parallel sharding work best when dimensions are multiples of 64, 128 or 256; 14,336 is $2^{11} \times 7$, divisible by every power of two up to 2,048. A hidden size of 10,922 would waste throughput on ragged tiles.

The ratio has drifted well away from 8/3 in recent models, which tells you it is a tunable budget, not a law. All values below are from each model's config.json (`intermediate_size` / `hidden_size`):

| Model | Activation | Width $d$ | FFN hidden | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Transformer base (2017) | ReLU | 512 | 2,048 | 4.0 |
| GPT-2 small | GELU (tanh) | 768 | 3,072 | 4.0 |
| Llama 2 7B | SwiGLU | 4,096 | 11,008 | 2.69 |
| Llama 3.1 8B | SwiGLU | 4,096 | 14,336 | 3.5 |
| Mistral 7B v0.1 | SwiGLU | 4,096 | 14,336 | 3.5 |
| Qwen2.5-7B | SwiGLU | 3,584 | 18,944 | 5.29 |
| Qwen3-8B | SwiGLU | 4,096 | 12,288 | 3.0 |
| Gemma 3 27B | GEGLU (tanh) | 5,376 | 21,504 | 4.0 |
| OLMo 2 7B | SwiGLU | 4,096 | 11,008 | 2.69 |
| DeepSeek-V3 (dense layers) | SwiGLU | 7,168 | 18,432 | 2.57 |
| DeepSeek-V3 (each routed expert) | SwiGLU | 7,168 | 2,048 | 0.29 |

The last row previews mixture-of-experts: DeepSeek-V3 replaces the FFN in 58 of its 61 layers with 256 small SwiGLU experts plus one shared expert, of which each token uses 8 routed experts. [Mixture of Experts](../7-llm-mixture-of-experts/0-llm-moe-introduction.mdx) covers how that works; each expert is an ordinary SwiGLU FFN like the one above.

## Worked Experiment: GELU vs SwiGLU at Matched Parameters

To see the effect yourself, train the same 4-layer, 128-wide character-level GPT on Tiny Shakespeare twice: once with a GELU FFN of hidden size $4d = 512$, once with SwiGLU of hidden size $\lfloor 8d/3 \rfloor = 341$. The two models have nearly identical parameter counts. Each is trained for 600 steps with AdamW, warmup and cosine decay, on two seeds, and evaluated on the same 40 validation batches.

```python
# Train the same tiny character-level GPT with a GELU MLP and with SwiGLU,
# matched in parameters, and compare validation loss.
import math, os, urllib.request
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.set_num_threads(8)
URL = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/karpathy/char-rnn/master/data/tinyshakespeare/input.txt"
if not os.path.exists("input.txt"):
    urllib.request.urlretrieve(URL, "input.txt")
text = open("input.txt").read()
chars = sorted(set(text)); stoi = {c: i for i, c in enumerate(chars)}
data = torch.tensor([stoi[c] for c in text]); n = int(0.9 * len(data))
train, val = data[:n], data[n:]
V, T, B, d, n_head, n_layer, steps = len(chars), 128, 32, 128, 4, 4, 600

class MLP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, kind):
        super().__init__()
        self.kind = kind
        hidden = 4 * d if kind == "gelu" else 8 * d // 3       # 512 vs 341: same parameter count
        self.up = nn.Linear(d, hidden, bias=False)
        self.gate = nn.Linear(d, hidden, bias=False) if kind == "swiglu" else None
        self.down = nn.Linear(hidden, d, bias=False)

    def forward(self, x):
        if self.kind == "gelu":
            return self.down(F.gelu(self.up(x)))
        return self.down(F.silu(self.gate(x)) * self.up(x))

class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, kind):
        super().__init__()
        self.ln1, self.ln2 = nn.LayerNorm(d, bias=False), nn.LayerNorm(d, bias=False)
        self.qkv, self.proj = nn.Linear(d, 3 * d, bias=False), nn.Linear(d, d, bias=False)
        self.mlp = MLP(kind)

    def forward(self, x):
        Bx, Tx, C = x.shape
        q, k, v = self.qkv(self.ln1(x)).view(Bx, Tx, 3, n_head, C // n_head).unbind(2)
        y = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q.transpose(1, 2), k.transpose(1, 2), v.transpose(1, 2), is_causal=True)
        x = x + self.proj(y.transpose(1, 2).reshape(Bx, Tx, C))
        return x + self.mlp(self.ln2(x))

class GPT(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, kind):
        super().__init__()
        self.tok, self.pos = nn.Embedding(V, d), nn.Embedding(T, d)
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList(Block(kind) for _ in range(n_layer))
        self.ln_f = nn.LayerNorm(d, bias=False)
        self.head = nn.Linear(d, V, bias=False); self.head.weight = self.tok.weight

    def forward(self, idx, tgt):
        x = self.tok(idx) + self.pos(torch.arange(idx.size(1)))
        for b in self.blocks:
            x = b(x)
        return F.cross_entropy(self.head(self.ln_f(x)).flatten(0, 1), tgt.flatten())

def batch(src, g):
    ix = torch.randint(len(src) - T - 1, (B,), generator=g)
    return torch.stack([src[i:i + T] for i in ix]), torch.stack([src[i + 1:i + 1 + T] for i in ix])

for kind in ("gelu", "swiglu"):
    finals = []
    for seed in (0, 1):
        torch.manual_seed(seed)
        model = GPT(kind)
        opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-3, betas=(0.9, 0.95), weight_decay=0.1)
        g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
        for step in range(steps):
            lr = 3e-3 * min(1, (step + 1) / 50) * (0.1 + 0.9 * 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * step / steps)))
            for group in opt.param_groups:
                group["lr"] = lr
            loss = model(*batch(train, g))
            opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
        model.eval()
        gv = torch.Generator().manual_seed(123)                   # same validation batches for every run
        with torch.no_grad():
            v = sum(model(*batch(val, gv)).item() for _ in range(40)) / 40
        finals.append(v)
    params = sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters())
    print(f"{kind:6s} | params {params:,} | val loss seed 0: {finals[0]:.3f}  seed 1: {finals[1]:.3f}"
          f"  mean: {sum(finals)/2:.3f}", flush=True)
```

```text
gelu   | params 812,288 | val loss seed 0: 2.300  seed 1: 2.289  mean: 2.295
swiglu | params 811,776 | val loss seed 0: 2.231  seed 1: 2.257  mean: 2.244
```

SwiGLU wins on both seeds, by about 0.05 nats on average (2.244 vs 2.295), with 512 fewer parameters (341 hidden units times three matrices is slightly less than 512 times two). That is the same direction and a similar size as Shazeer's 0.04-nat gap, in a model a hundred times smaller trained for only 600 steps. The seed-to-seed spread (0.011 for GELU, 0.026 for SwiGLU) is smaller than the gap, but two seeds is a thin basis: rerun with more seeds and longer training before drawing firm conclusions. The whole script took about an hour of wall-clock time on a heavily shared 32-core machine; on an idle laptop expect several minutes per run.

## Common Mistakes

- **Swapping gate and up projections when loading weights.** In SwiGLU, only `gate_proj` goes through the activation. Applying SiLU to `up_proj` instead gives a model that loads cleanly and generates nonsense.
- **Keeping $d_\mathrm{ff} = 4d$ when switching to SwiGLU.** That makes the FFN 50% larger than the GELU baseline. If you compare architectures, match parameters with $h = 8d/3$ (rounded), or say explicitly that you did not.
- **Mixing up exact and tanh GELU.** `F.gelu(x)` is exact; `F.gelu(x, approximate="tanh")` is GPT-2's version and the one Gemma uses. Hugging Face config strings (`gelu`, `gelu_new`, `gelu_pytorch_tanh`) name different functions.
- **Choosing an unaligned hidden size.** A hidden width like 10,922 runs noticeably slower on GPUs than 11,008. Round to a multiple of at least 128, and check divisibility by your tensor-parallel degree.
- **Expecting the activation alone to matter.** Changing ReLU to GELU barely moves perplexity; gating is the part that helps. When reading ablations, separate the two.
- **Forgetting that the FFN dominates FLOPs.** Optimizations that ignore the FFN (only speeding up attention) leave most of the short-context compute untouched.

## Summary

- The FFN applies the same two-layer network to every position; with $d_\mathrm{ff} = 4d$ it has $8d^2$ weights, two thirds of a classic block.
- GELU ($z\Phi(z)$) and SiLU ($z\sigma(z)$) are smooth replacements for ReLU; on their own they change quality only slightly.
- Gated units multiply an activated projection with a linear one; SwiGLU and GEGLU gave the best perplexity in Shazeer's matched experiments (1.636 and 1.633 vs 1.677 for ReLU) and are now standard.
- Matching parameters with three matrices gives $h = 8d/3$; Meta's code takes 2/3 of $4d$, applies an optional multiplier and rounds up, producing 11,008 for Llama 2 7B and 14,336 (3.5x) for Llama 3 8B.
- Real ratios range from about 2.6 to over 5; mixture-of-experts models use many small SwiGLU experts instead of one large FFN.
