# Small Language Models and On-Device Inference

## Introduction

The largest models get the headlines, but a growing share of LLM inference runs on models with between 100 million and 4 billion parameters. They run on phones, laptops, browsers and embedded boards, with no server involved. The reasons are practical:

- **Privacy**: the text never leaves the device.
- **Latency and offline use**: no network round trip, and the feature still works on a plane.
- **Cost**: the user's hardware does the work, so there is no per-token bill.
- **Specialization**: a small model fine-tuned for one task, such as summarizing notifications, extracting fields or routing requests, can match a much larger general model on that task.

Small models used to be weak. Modern ones are much better, not because of a single trick but because of changes to how they are trained and shaped. This page covers those changes, the hardware limits of on-device inference, and the tools to run models locally. By the end you will be able to:

- explain why small models are trained on far more tokens than the Chinchilla rule suggests;
- describe how distillation and pruning produce small models from large ones;
- read a small model's `config.json` and see where its parameters go, including the surprisingly large embedding share;
- estimate a model's memory footprint and decode speed on a given device, and pick a model and quantization for a memory budget;
- choose a runtime for on-device deployment.

## The Hardware Budget: Memory and Bandwidth

Two numbers decide what runs on a device.

**Memory capacity** sets the largest model you can load. A phone shares its RAM between the operating system, other apps and yours, so a single app may get only a few GB. Laptops have more, and Apple Silicon machines share one pool of unified memory between CPU and GPU, so a 32 GB MacBook can hold a model that would not fit on a 16 GB discrete GPU.

**Memory bandwidth** sets the decode speed. During decoding, every generated token reads all the active weights once (see [Prefill and Decode](../10-llm-inference/0-llm-inference-overview.mdx)), so:

$$
\text{tokens per second} \lesssim \frac{\text{memory bandwidth (bytes/s)}}{\text{bytes of weights read per token}}
$$

For a 1B-parameter model at about 4.9 bits per weight (llama.cpp's `Q4_K_M`), the weights are about 0.6 GB. A device with 100 GB/s of bandwidth is then capped at roughly 160 tokens per second, and a device with 50 GB/s at roughly 80. Real speeds come in below these bounds, but the proportions hold. This is why quantization matters even more on-device than in the data centre: halving the bits roughly doubles the speed, as well as halving the memory.

**Prefill**, processing the prompt, is different. It is compute-bound, so it runs best on a GPU or a neural processing unit (NPU), and a long prompt can take noticeably longer than generating a short answer. **Power and heat** add a third limit on phones: sustained generation can cause thermal throttling, so the speed you measure in the first ten seconds may not hold for a minute.

## How Small Models Got Good

### Over-Training Past Chinchilla

The Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022) says that for a fixed **training** budget, the best model uses about 20 tokens per parameter (see [Scaling Laws](../6-llm-pretraining/2-llm-scaling-laws.mdx)). But that rule minimizes training cost only. A model deployed to millions of devices runs inference far more often than it is trained, so it pays to train a smaller model much longer: the extra training is paid once, and the cheaper inference is enjoyed forever. Loss keeps improving with more tokens, just more slowly.

Small models now take this to extremes:

| Model | Parameters | Training tokens | Tokens per parameter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chinchilla rule of thumb | any | 20 per parameter | 20 |
| SmolLM2-135M | 135M | 2T | about 15,000 |
| Gemma 3 270M | 268M | 6T | about 22,000 |
| Gemma 3 1B | 1.0B | 2T | about 2,000 |
| Llama 3.2 1B | 1.24B | up to 9T | about 7,000 |
| SmolLM3-3B | 3.1B | 11.2T | about 3,600 |
| Qwen3 (all sizes) | 0.6B to 235B | 36T | 60,000 for the 0.6B model |

The token counts come from each model's card or technical report. A Chinchilla-optimal 1B model would see only 20 billion tokens. Llama 3.2 1B saw hundreds of times more.

### Distillation and Pruning

Training on more tokens helps, but each token gives only one "correct" next token. **Knowledge distillation** gives the small model the full probability distribution of a large teacher model as the target, which carries far more information per token (see [Knowledge Distillation](../8-llm-post-training/5-llm-distillation.mdx)). Meta's Llama 3.2 1B and 3B models used the logits of Llama 3.1 8B and 70B as token-level targets during pretraining, and Google trains all Gemma 3 models with distillation.

**Structured pruning** starts from a large trained model and removes whole layers, attention heads, MLP neurons or embedding channels, then retrains briefly with distillation to recover. NVIDIA's Minitron work (Muralidharan et al., 2024) compressed Nemotron-4 15B into 8B and 4B models using less than 3% of the original training tokens for the retraining. Llama 3.2 1B and 3B were also produced by pruning Llama 3.1 8B, followed by distillation. Pruning plus distillation is often far cheaper than training a small model from scratch.

### Data Quality

Microsoft's Phi series argued that a small model's capacity is too precious to waste on low-quality web text. Phi-3-mini (Abdin et al., 2024), with 3.8B parameters trained on 3.3T tokens of heavily filtered web data and synthetic "textbook-like" data, reported 69% on MMLU, comparable to much larger models of its time. The report also showed it running fully offline on an iPhone at more than 12 tokens per second after 4-bit quantization, at about 1.8 GB. Careful filtering and synthetic data are now standard in all small-model recipes (see [Pretraining Data](../6-llm-pretraining/0-llm-pretraining-data.mdx)).

## Architecture Choices at Small Scale

### Deep and Thin, with Shared Embeddings

MobileLLM (Liu et al., 2024) studied models under 1 billion parameters and found that, for a fixed parameter count, **deep and thin** beats wide and shallow: their 125M model has 30 layers. They also recommended **embedding sharing** (tying the input embedding and output projection matrices) and grouped-query attention. All three choices show up in today's small models, as the configs in the next script show.

### Where the Parameters Go

In a large model the embedding matrix is a rounding error. In a small model it can be most of the model. The embedding has $V \times d$ parameters for vocabulary size $V$ and model dimension $d$, while each transformer layer has roughly $12 d^2$ (attention plus MLP, see [Counting Parameters](../3-llm-transformer/6-llm-parameter-and-flop-counting.mdx)). When $d$ is small and $V$ is large, the embedding wins. The script below builds each model on PyTorch's `meta` device, which creates the module structure without allocating memory, so only the small `config.json` files are downloaded:

```python
import torch
from transformers import AutoConfig, AutoModelForCausalLM

torch.manual_seed(0)
models = ["HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-135M", "unsloth/gemma-3-270m-it", "Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B",
          "unsloth/gemma-3-1b-it", "unsloth/Llama-3.2-1B", "Qwen/Qwen3-1.7B", "HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM3-3B"]

print(f"{'model':<28}{'total':>8}{'embed':>8}{'embed %':>9}{'layers':>8}{'d_model':>9}{'vocab':>8}")
for name in models:
    cfg = AutoConfig.from_pretrained(name)
    with torch.device("meta"):                                     # build the graph without allocating weights
        model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_config(cfg)
    total = sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters())             # tied embeddings are counted once
    embed = model.get_input_embeddings().weight.numel()
    tc = getattr(cfg, "text_config", cfg)
    print(f"{name.split('/')[-1]:<28}{total/1e6:>7.0f}M{embed/1e6:>7.0f}M{100*embed/total:>8.0f}%"
          f"{tc.num_hidden_layers:>8}{tc.hidden_size:>9}{tc.vocab_size:>8}")
```

```text
model                          total   embed  embed %  layers  d_model   vocab
SmolLM2-135M                    135M     28M      21%      30      576   49152
gemma-3-270m-it                 268M    168M      63%      18      640  262144
Qwen3-0.6B                      596M    156M      26%      28     1024  151936
gemma-3-1b-it                  1000M    302M      30%      26     1152  262144
Llama-3.2-1B                   1236M    263M      21%      16     2048  128256
Qwen3-1.7B                     1721M    311M      18%      28     2048  151936
SmolLM3-3B                     3075M    263M       9%      36     2048  128256
```

(The `unsloth` repositories are ungated mirrors with the same configs as the official Google and Meta ones.)

Gemma 3 270M is the extreme case: 63% of its parameters are the embedding table for its 262,144-token vocabulary, and only about 100M are in the transformer layers. Google made that choice deliberately. A large vocabulary handles many languages and rare tokens well, which helps a model meant to be fine-tuned for specific tasks. All of these models tie their embeddings (the Gemma configs do so by default), since an untied output matrix would add another full $V \times d$ block.

The embedding share matters for deployment in two ways. An embedding lookup reads only one row per token, so most of the embedding's bytes are not read during decoding, and memory use exceeds what the bandwidth formula would suggest. But with tied weights the same matrix is also the output projection, which **is** read in full for every token. Low-bit recipes often keep it at a higher precision, because errors there act directly on the output logits.

### Other Tricks

- **Sliding-window layers**: Gemma 3 1B and 270M use a 512-token sliding window in most layers, with a full-attention layer every sixth layer, which keeps the KV cache small.
- **Cross-layer KV sharing**: Apple's roughly 3B on-device model (2025) shares the KV cache of its first block of layers with the second block, cutting cache memory by 37.5% and also skipping the second block's work during prefill.
- **Per-layer embeddings and nested models**: Gemma 3n stores large per-layer embedding tables that can stay in flash storage, and uses a MatFormer ("Matryoshka") design in which a smaller model is nested inside a larger one. Its E2B variant has over 5 billion parameters in total, but loads only about 1.9 billion into working memory.

## Quantization for Devices: PTQ, QAT and Ternary Weights

Everything in this section applies to small models, with one caveat: **small models are more sensitive to quantization**. Each weight carries more of the model's knowledge, so the same rounding hurts more (the 135M-parameter experiment in [Quantization Basics](./0-llm-quantization-basics.mdx) lost far more perplexity at 4 bits than a 70B model would). Three responses are common:

- **Higher-precision formats for small models.** `Q5_K_M`, `Q6_K` or `Q8_0` cost little in absolute terms for a 1B model, and they keep more quality.
- **Quantization-aware training (QAT).** Training with fake quantization in the loop lets the model adapt to the rounding. Google released QAT versions of Gemma 3 for the `Q4_0` format. Its blog post reports that QAT cut the perplexity drop from quantizing to `Q4_0` by 54%, while the 27B model's weights shrank from 54 GB in BF16 to 14.1 GB. Meta released Llama 3.2 1B and 3B variants made with QAT plus LoRA, and with SpinQuant. Apple's 2025 on-device model uses 2-bit QAT.
- **Ternary models trained from scratch.** BitNet b1.58 (Ma et al., 2024) restricts every weight to $\lbrace -1, 0, 1 \rbrace$, which carries $\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits of information. Matrix multiplies become additions and subtractions. Microsoft's BitNet b1.58 2B4T (2025) is a 2-billion-parameter model trained on 4 trillion tokens this way, with its own CPU runtime, `bitnet.cpp`. Ternary weights cannot be applied to an existing model after the fact. They have to be trained in from the start.

## A Worked Scenario: Choosing a Model for a 3 GB Budget

Suppose you are building a mobile app that summarizes documents and the platform lets your app use about 3 GB. You need 4,096 tokens of context. Which models fit at `Q4_K_M`? The script reads each model's config, counts parameters, and adds up weights, an FP16 KV cache at 4,096 tokens, and about 0.3 GB of compute buffers:

```python
import torch
from transformers import AutoConfig, AutoModelForCausalLM

torch.manual_seed(0)
BPW = 4.89                       # llama.cpp Q4_K_M, measured on Llama 3.1 8B
CTX = 4096                       # context the app needs
BUDGET_GB = 3.0                  # memory the OS lets the app use

print(f"{'model':<22}{'params':>8}{'weights':>10}{'KV/token':>10}{'KV@4K':>8}{'total':>8}  fits?")
for name in ["Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B", "unsloth/Llama-3.2-1B", "Qwen/Qwen3-1.7B",
             "HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM3-3B", "microsoft/Phi-4-mini-instruct"]:
    cfg = AutoConfig.from_pretrained(name)
    with torch.device("meta"):
        n = sum(p.numel() for p in AutoModelForCausalLM.from_config(cfg).parameters())
    head_dim = getattr(cfg, "head_dim", None) or cfg.hidden_size // cfg.num_attention_heads
    kv_tok = 2 * cfg.num_hidden_layers * cfg.num_key_value_heads * head_dim * 2   # FP16 cache, bytes
    w_gb = n * BPW / 8 / 1e9
    kv_gb = kv_tok * CTX / 1e9
    total = w_gb + kv_gb + 0.3                                    # about 0.3 GB of compute buffers
    print(f"{name.split('/')[-1]:<22}{n/1e9:>7.2f}B{w_gb:>9.2f}G{kv_tok/1024:>8.0f}KiB{kv_gb:>7.2f}G"
          f"{total:>7.2f}G  {'yes' if total <= BUDGET_GB else 'no'}")
```

```text
model                   params   weights  KV/token   KV@4K   total  fits?
Qwen3-0.6B               0.60B     0.36G     112KiB   0.47G   1.13G  yes
Llama-3.2-1B             1.24B     0.76G      32KiB   0.13G   1.19G  yes
Qwen3-1.7B               1.72B     1.05G     112KiB   0.47G   1.82G  yes
SmolLM3-3B               3.08B     1.88G      72KiB   0.30G   2.48G  yes
Phi-4-mini-instruct      3.84B     2.34G     128KiB   0.54G   3.18G  no
```

Some lessons from the table:

- **The KV cache varies a lot between models of similar size.** Qwen3-0.6B needs 112 KiB per token (28 layers, 8 KV heads of dimension 128), while the twice-as-large Llama 3.2 1B needs only 32 KiB (16 layers, 8 KV heads of dimension 64). At 4K tokens the cache is bigger than Qwen3-0.6B's weights.
- **SmolLM3-3B fits with room to spare.** It is the largest model that fits, and usually the best choice of these for quality. Phi-4-mini would need an 8-bit KV cache, a shorter context or a smaller weight format (for example `IQ4_XS` at 4.25 bits per weight) to fit.
- **The budget is not the whole story.** Speed matters too: at about 50 GB/s of bandwidth, SmolLM3-3B's 1.9 GB of weights allow at most about 26 tokens per second, while Llama 3.2 1B could reach about 65. For a summarization feature that writes a few hundred tokens, either may be fine. For an interactive assistant, the smaller model may feel better.

In a real project you would then fine-tune the shortlisted models on your task (see [LoRA](../9-llm-fine-tuning/1-llm-lora.mdx)), quantize them, and measure quality and speed on the actual device. A fine-tuned 1B model often beats an untuned 3B one on a narrow task.

## Measuring Decode Speed on Your Machine

The bandwidth bound is easy to check. This script times greedy generation of SmolLM2-135M on the CPU in FP32 and BF16, and converts the speed into the bandwidth of weight reads it implies:

```python
import time, torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

torch.manual_seed(0)
torch.set_num_threads(4)
NAME = "HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-135M"
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(NAME)
inputs = tok("Small language models are useful because", return_tensors="pt")

for dtype in (torch.float32, torch.bfloat16):
    model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(NAME, dtype=dtype).eval()
    weight_bytes = sum(p.numel() * p.element_size() for p in model.parameters())
    model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=8, do_sample=False)            # warm-up
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    out = model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=64, min_new_tokens=64, do_sample=False)
    dt = time.perf_counter() - t0
    n = out.shape[1] - inputs.input_ids.shape[1]
    print(f"{str(dtype):<16} weights {weight_bytes/1e6:6.0f} MB  {n/dt:6.1f} tokens/s  "
          f"-> {weight_bytes * n / dt / 1e9:5.1f} GB/s of weight reads")
print(repr(tok.decode(out[0, inputs.input_ids.shape[1]:][:24])))
```

```text
torch.float32    weights    538 MB     7.3 tokens/s  ->   3.9 GB/s of weight reads
torch.bfloat16   weights    269 MB    17.0 tokens/s  ->   4.6 GB/s of weight reads
' they can be trained to recognize patterns in large amounts of text. For example, a text-to-speech system can'
```

This run used 4 CPU threads on a Linux server, so your numbers will differ. Two things show up. Halving the bytes per weight (FP32 to BF16) made generation about 2.3 times faster, the direction the bandwidth bound predicts. And the implied bandwidth, a few GB/s, is far below what any modern CPU's memory can deliver. For a model this small, the time per token is dominated by fixed overheads, such as Python, the `generate` loop and launching about 30 layers' worth of small kernels, rather than by reading weights. That is the other reason on-device runtimes are written in C++ with fused kernels: at small model sizes, removing overhead matters as much as quantization.

## Runtimes for On-Device Inference

| Runtime | Platforms | Model format | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| llama.cpp | CPU, CUDA, Metal, Vulkan, Android, iOS | GGUF | the broadest hardware support; also the engine behind many desktop apps |
| Ollama, LM Studio | macOS, Windows, Linux | GGUF and others | model management and a local API on top of llama.cpp-style engines |
| MLX and mlx-lm | Apple Silicon | MLX safetensors | Apple's array framework; `mlx_lm.convert -q` quantizes, `mlx_lm.generate` runs |
| Apple Foundation Models framework | iOS, iPadOS, macOS | built-in model | gives apps access to Apple's on-device model, no download needed |
| ExecuTorch | iOS, Android, embedded | `.pte` exported from PyTorch | PyTorch's on-device runtime, with CPU, GPU and NPU backends |
| LiteRT-LM | Android, iOS, desktop, web | LiteRT models | Google's edge LLM runtime, used for Gemma models |
| ONNX Runtime GenAI | Windows, Android, iOS, web | ONNX | Microsoft's generative runtime, with DirectML and NPU support |
| WebLLM | browsers with WebGPU | MLC-compiled | runs models fully in the browser tab |
| bitnet.cpp | CPU | ternary GGUF | for BitNet b1.58 models |

For a laptop prototype, llama.cpp, Ollama or (on a Mac) mlx-lm are the fastest ways to start:

```bash
# Apple Silicon: download, quantize to 4 bits and run with MLX
pip install mlx-lm
mlx_lm.convert --model Qwen/Qwen3-1.7B -q
mlx_lm.generate --model mlx_model --prompt "Summarize: small models run on phones because"

# Any platform: run a ready-made GGUF with llama.cpp
llama-cli -hf ggml-org/Qwen3-1.7B-GGUF -c 4096
```

For a shipping mobile app, the platform-native runtimes (Apple's framework, ExecuTorch, LiteRT-LM) give better access to the NPU and better power efficiency, at the cost of a model conversion step.

:::note
The NPU is good at the compute-bound prefill phase, but decode is limited by memory bandwidth, which the NPU shares with the CPU and GPU. Moving decode to the NPU usually saves power more than it adds speed.
:::

## Common Mistakes

- **Choosing a model by parameter count alone.** Two models of similar size can differ several times in KV cache size and in how much of their weights the embedding takes up. Read the config.
- **Quantizing a tiny model as aggressively as a big one.** A 4-bit 135M model loses much more than a 4-bit 70B model. Prefer 5 to 8 bits for sub-billion models, or a QAT checkpoint.
- **Ignoring the KV cache on device.** At a few thousand tokens, the cache of a small model can exceed its weights.
- **Benchmarking only the first seconds.** Phones throttle under sustained load. Measure a realistic session.
- **Measuring speed on an idle desktop and assuming the phone will match.** The bandwidth formula tells you what to expect: scale your measured speed by the ratio of memory bandwidths.
- **Expecting general knowledge from a tiny model.** Small models have little room to store facts. Give them the facts in the prompt (see [RAG and agents](/docs/ai/agents)) or fine-tune them for a narrow task.

## Summary

- On-device LLMs trade raw capability for privacy, latency, offline use and zero serving cost. Memory capacity limits model size, and memory bandwidth limits decode speed: tokens per second is at most bandwidth divided by the bytes of weights read per token.
- Small models are trained far past the Chinchilla optimum (thousands of tokens per parameter), with distillation from larger teachers, structured pruning and heavily filtered or synthetic data.
- Small models favour deep and thin shapes, tied embeddings and GQA. Their embedding can be a large share of the parameters, 63% in Gemma 3 270M.
- Small models are more sensitive to quantization. Use higher-precision formats, QAT checkpoints, or models trained with low-bit weights from the start, such as BitNet b1.58.
- Budget memory as weights plus KV cache plus buffers. On a 3 GB budget at 4K context and `Q4_K_M`, models up to about 3B parameters fit.
- llama.cpp, Ollama and mlx-lm are the fastest way to prototype. Apple's Foundation Models framework, ExecuTorch and LiteRT-LM are the platform runtimes for shipping apps.
