# Scaling Laws: Kaplan, Chinchilla and Beyond

## Introduction

Before spending millions of dollars of GPU time, a team has to answer two questions: how big should the model be, and how many tokens should it see? Scaling laws answer them. They are empirical formulas, fitted on dozens of small training runs, that predict the final loss of a much larger run from its parameter count $N$, its number of training tokens $D$ and its training compute $C$.

This page derives the main results instead of only quoting them. You will see Kaplan et al.'s 2020 power laws, the Chinchilla correction of 2022 and the "20 tokens per parameter" rule that came out of it, the closed-form compute-optimal allocation, the IsoFLOP method in a short NumPy script, and why every popular open model since Llama trains far past the Chinchilla point. By the end you can read the scaling section of a model report and redo its arithmetic.

## Compute, Parameters and Tokens

Everything rests on one approximation: training a dense Transformer costs

$$
C \approx 6 N D
$$

floating-point operations. The forward pass does about $2N$ FLOPs per token (one multiply and one add per weight), and the backward pass about twice that, $4N$, because it computes gradients for both activations and weights. The approximation ignores attention's $T^2$ term, which is small when the model width is large compared with the context length. The [FLOP counting page](../3-llm-transformer/6-llm-parameter-and-flop-counting.mdx) derives it layer by layer.

Numeric check: Chinchilla has $N = 70 \times 10^9$ parameters and saw $D = 1.4 \times 10^{12}$ tokens, so $C = 6 \cdot 70 \times 10^9 \cdot 1.4 \times 10^{12} = 5.88 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs. Gopher, with 280B parameters on 300B tokens, used $5.04 \times 10^{23}$: almost the same budget, spent very differently.

For a fixed budget $C$ you can trade $N$ against $D$: a bigger model sees fewer tokens. A scaling law says which trade minimizes the loss.

## Kaplan et al. (2020): Power Laws Everywhere

Jared Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI ("Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models", 2020) trained models from under a thousand to about 1.5 billion non-embedding parameters on WebText2. They found that the test loss follows a power law in each resource when the other two are not the bottleneck:

$$
L(N) = \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N}, \qquad L(D) = \left(\frac{D_c}{D}\right)^{\alpha_D}
$$

with $\alpha_N \approx 0.076$, $N_c \approx 8.8 \times 10^{13}$ non-embedding parameters, $\alpha_D \approx 0.095$ and $D_c \approx 5.4 \times 10^{13}$ tokens. A power law is a straight line on a log-log plot, which is why loss-versus-compute plots in papers use logarithmic axes. The small exponents mean slow progress: doubling $N$ multiplies the loss by $2^{-0.076} = 0.949$, a 5% reduction.

Their compute-efficient frontier gave $N_\mathrm{opt} \propto C^{0.73}$ and $D_\mathrm{opt} \propto C^{0.27}$: when compute grows ten times, make the model 5.4 times bigger but train on only 1.9 times more data. That recommendation shaped the models of 2020 and 2021. GPT-3 had 175B parameters and 300B training tokens, under two tokens per parameter.

Two details of the method mattered later. Kaplan et al. counted only non-embedding parameters, and they used one learning rate schedule length for all runs, so smaller runs were stopped part way through a schedule that was tuned for a longer one.

## Chinchilla (2022): Scale Data with Parameters

Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues at DeepMind ("Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models", 2022) trained over 400 models from 70M to over 16B parameters on 5B to 500B tokens, and matched the cosine schedule length to each run's token count. They estimated the optimal allocation three independent ways:

1. **Minimum over training curves**: for each FLOP count, take the lowest loss reached by any model.
2. **IsoFLOP profiles**: for each of nine fixed budgets, train models of several sizes, fit a parabola to loss versus $\log N$ and take its minimum.
3. **Parametric fit**: fit one formula for $L(N, D)$ to all runs.

| Approach | $a$ in $N_\mathrm{opt} \propto C^a$ | $b$ in $D_\mathrm{opt} \propto C^b$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Training curve envelope | 0.50 | 0.50 |
| 2. IsoFLOP profiles | 0.49 | 0.51 |
| 3. Parametric loss | 0.46 | 0.54 |
| Kaplan et al. (2020) | 0.73 | 0.27 |

All three say: grow parameters and tokens at the same rate. At the Gopher budget the optimal model was about 70B parameters on about 1.4T tokens, so they trained exactly that model, Chinchilla, and it beat the four times larger Gopher on nearly every benchmark (67.5% on 5-shot MMLU, more than 7 points above Gopher). The ratio $D/N = 1.4 \times 10^{12} / 7 \times 10^{10} = 20$ became the famous rule of thumb: **about 20 tokens per parameter** is compute-optimal.

Why did Kaplan get a different answer? Two later papers traced the gap to method rather than nature. Pearce and Song (2024) showed that counting non-embedding parameters at small scale biases the fitted exponent toward Kaplan's 0.73. Porian et al. (2024) reproduced Kaplan's result and then removed it by fixing three things: the compute of the final output layer, the warmup length, and the optimizer settings that should change with scale. With those fixed, their results agreed closely with Chinchilla.

## Deriving the Compute-Optimal Allocation

Approach 3 fits this functional form:

$$
L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}
$$

Each term has a meaning. $E$ is the irreducible loss, the entropy of natural text that no model can remove. $A / N^\alpha$ is the penalty for a finite model: even with infinite data, a small model cannot represent everything. $B / D^\beta$ is the penalty for finite data: even an infinite model, trained on a finite sample, has not seen everything.

Now minimize $L$ subject to $C = 6ND$. Substitute $D = C / (6N)$:

$$
L(N) = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B \left(\frac{6N}{C}\right)^\beta
$$

Set the derivative with respect to $N$ to zero:

$$
-\alpha A N^{-\alpha - 1} + \beta B \left(\frac{6}{C}\right)^\beta N^{\beta - 1} = 0
$$

Multiply by $N$ and rearrange: $\alpha A N^{-\alpha} = \beta B (6N/C)^\beta$. At the optimum, the two penalty terms are in the fixed ratio $\beta : \alpha$. Solving for $N$:

$$
N_\mathrm{opt} = G \left(\frac{C}{6}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}}, \qquad
D_\mathrm{opt} = \frac{C}{6 N_\mathrm{opt}}, \qquad
G = \left(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha + \beta}}
$$

The exponent $a = \beta / (\alpha + \beta)$ explains everything in the table above: when $\alpha \approx \beta$, $a \approx 0.5$ and parameters and data grow together.

The paper reported $E = 1.69$, $A = 406.4$, $B = 410.7$, $\alpha = 0.34$, $\beta = 0.28$. In 2024, Besiroglu et al. ("Chinchilla Scaling: A Replication Attempt") reconstructed the data points from the paper's figure, refitted the same formula and got $E = 1.8172$, $A = 482.01$, $B = 2085.43$, $\alpha = 0.3478$, $\beta = 0.3658$. They showed that the published Approach 3 values did not fit the data well and were inconsistent with Approaches 1 and 2, while their refit implied about 20 tokens per parameter, consistent with how Chinchilla was actually trained. The script evaluates both fits:

```python
import numpy as np

# Parametric fits L(N, D) = E + A / N**alpha + B / D**beta
FITS = {
    "Hoffmann 2022":  dict(E=1.69,   A=406.4,  B=410.7,   alpha=0.34,   beta=0.28),
    "Besiroglu 2024": dict(E=1.8172, A=482.01, B=2085.43, alpha=0.3478, beta=0.3658),
}

def loss(N, D, E, A, B, alpha, beta):
    return E + A / N**alpha + B / D**beta

def optimum(C, E, A, B, alpha, beta):
    """Minimize L(N, D) subject to C = 6 N D (closed form from the Lagrangian)."""
    G = (alpha * A / (beta * B)) ** (1 / (alpha + beta))
    N = G * (C / 6) ** (beta / (alpha + beta))
    D = C / (6 * N)
    return N, D

for name, f in FITS.items():
    a = f["beta"] / (f["alpha"] + f["beta"])
    print(f"{name}: N_opt ~ C^{a:.2f}, D_opt ~ C^{1 - a:.2f}")
    for C in [1e21, 5.76e23, 1e25]:
        N, D = optimum(C, **f)
        print(f"  C={C:.2e}  N={N / 1e9:7.1f}B  D={D / 1e12:6.2f}T  D/N={D / N:5.1f}  "
              f"L={loss(N, D, **f):.3f}")

f = FITS["Besiroglu 2024"]
for model, N, D in [("Gopher", 280e9, 300e9), ("Chinchilla", 70e9, 1.4e12)]:
    print(f"{model:10s} C=6ND={6 * N * D:.2e}  predicted loss {loss(N, D, **f):.3f}")
```

```text
  C=5.76e+23  N=   32.2B  D=  2.98T  D/N= 92.6  L=1.931
  C=1.00e+25  N=  116.8B  D= 14.27T  D/N=122.1  L=1.845
Besiroglu 2024: N_opt ~ C^0.51, D_opt ~ C^0.49
  C=1.00e+21  N=    2.8B  D=  0.06T  D/N= 21.6  L=2.306
  C=5.76e+23  N=   72.2B  D=  1.33T  D/N= 18.4  L=1.974
  C=1.00e+25  N=  312.1B  D=  5.34T  D/N= 17.1  L=1.912
Gopher     C=6ND=5.04e+23  predicted loss 2.000

```

The published fit recommends 93 tokens per parameter at the Chinchilla budget and gives the exponent 0.45, which matches its own Approach 3 row but contradicts Approaches 1 and 2. The refit gives 17 to 22 tokens per parameter, a 72B model on 1.33T tokens at the Chinchilla budget, which is almost exactly the real Chinchilla. Under the refit law, Chinchilla's predicted loss (1.974) is lower than Gopher's (2.000) with a similar budget. The lesson goes beyond Chinchilla: fitted constants are fragile, and a small error in an exponent changes the extrapolation by orders of magnitude.

## The IsoFLOP Method in Practice

Approach 2 is the one most labs reuse, because it needs no assumed formula for $L(N, D)$: pick a few budgets, train a handful of sizes at each, find each minimum, and fit a line through the minima on a log-log plot. The script simulates that experiment, using the refitted law plus noise as a stand-in for real training runs:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
E, A, B, alpha, beta = 1.8172, 482.01, 2085.43, 0.3478, 0.3658   # Besiroglu et al. fit

def loss(N, D):
    return E + A / N**alpha + B / D**beta

# Approach 2 (IsoFLOP profiles): for each budget, train several sizes, fit a parabola in log N.
budgets = [1e18, 1e19, 1e20, 1e21]
best_N = []
for C in budgets:
    N = np.logspace(7, 10.5, 15)                      # 10M .. 30B parameters
    D = C / (6 * N)
    L = loss(N, D) + rng.normal(0, 0.005, N.size)     # pretend training noise
    keep = np.argsort(L)[:7]                          # fit only around the minimum
    c2, c1, c0 = np.polyfit(np.log(N[keep]), L[keep], 2)
    n_star = np.exp(-c1 / (2 * c2))                   # vertex of the parabola
    best_N.append(n_star)
    print(f"C={C:.0e}  best N={n_star / 1e6:8.1f}M  tokens={C / (6 * n_star) / 1e9:7.2f}B  "
          f"tokens/param={C / (6 * n_star) / n_star:5.1f}")

slope, intercept = np.polyfit(np.log(budgets), np.log(best_N), 1)
print(f"fitted N_opt ~ C^{slope:.3f}   (closed form: {beta / (alpha + beta):.3f})")
N_big = np.exp(intercept) * 5.76e23**slope
print(f"extrapolated to 5.76e23 FLOPs: N={N_big / 1e9:.1f}B, D={5.76e23 / (6 * N_big) / 1e12:.2f}T")
```

```text
C=1e+18  best N=    79.4M  tokens=   2.10B  tokens/param= 26.4
C=1e+19  best N=   260.4M  tokens=   6.40B  tokens/param= 24.6
C=1e+20  best N=   836.6M  tokens=  19.92B  tokens/param= 23.8
C=1e+21  best N=  2767.4M  tokens=  60.23B  tokens/param= 21.8
fitted N_opt ~ C^0.513   (closed form: 0.513)
extrapolated to 5.76e23 FLOPs: N=72.0B, D=1.33T
```

Four small budgets (the largest, $10^{21}$ FLOPs, is about four days on one node of eight H100 GPUs at 40% utilization) recover the exponent and predict the 72B, 1.33T-token optimum at a budget 576 times larger. Real experiments are noisier, so labs use more budgets and repeat runs.

Llama 3 is the best-documented modern example. Meta ran IsoFLOP experiments from $6 \times 10^{18}$ to $10^{22}$ FLOPs on models of 40M to 16B parameters, fitted the optimal token count as $D^\ast(C) = A C^\alpha$ with $\alpha = 0.53$ and $A = 0.29$, and extrapolated to their $3.8 \times 10^{25}$ FLOP budget: 16.55T tokens, which implies about 402B parameters. They trained a 405B model on 15.6T tokens. They also observed that the IsoFLOP curves flatten near the minimum at large budgets, so being off by a modest factor in model size costs little loss. The same report predicted downstream benchmark accuracy in two steps (compute to benchmark negative log-likelihood, then log-likelihood to accuracy) and hit the 405B model's ARC-Challenge score closely.

## Beyond Chinchilla: Over-Training for Inference

Chinchilla-optimal means the lowest loss for a given training budget. Nobody deploys a training budget. They deploy a model, and every generated token costs about $2N$ FLOPs. A smaller model trained longer reaches the same loss at a higher training cost but a lower serving cost.

| Model | Parameters | Training tokens | Tokens per parameter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GPT-3 (2020) | 175B | 300B | 1.7 |
| Chinchilla (2022) | 70B | 1.4T | 20 |
| Llama 2 7B (2023) | 7B | 2T | 286 |
| Llama 3 8B (2024) | 8B | about 15T | about 1,900 |
| Qwen3 0.6B to 235B (2025) | 0.6B to 235B | about 36T | up to 60,000 |

Sardana et al. (2023, "Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal: Accounting for Inference in Language Model Scaling Laws") formalized the trade-off: if you expect large inference demand, you should train a smaller model on more data than the Chinchilla rule says. You can reproduce their argument with the fitted law: fix a target loss, and among all $(N, D)$ pairs that reach it, choose the one that minimizes training plus inference FLOPs, $6ND + 2N D_\mathrm{inf}$, where $D_\mathrm{inf}$ is the number of tokens the model will generate over its life.

```python
import numpy as np

E, A, B, alpha, beta = 1.8172, 482.01, 2085.43, 0.3478, 0.3658   # Besiroglu et al. fit

def tokens_needed(N, target):
    """Training tokens D that bring a model of size N to the target loss (inf if impossible)."""
    gap = target - E - A / N**alpha
    return np.where(gap > 0, (B / np.maximum(gap, 1e-12)) ** (1 / beta), np.inf)

N_ref, D_ref = 7e9, 7e9 * 20                       # a Chinchilla-style 7B model, 140B tokens
target = E + A / N_ref**alpha + B / D_ref**beta
print(f"target loss {target:.4f} (7B params, 140B tokens)")

N = np.logspace(9, 11, 2000)                       # candidate sizes 1B .. 100B
D = tokens_needed(N, target)
for served in [0, 1e11, 1e12, 1e13]:              # tokens generated over the model's lifetime
    cost = 6 * N * D + 2 * N * served             # training + inference FLOPs
    i = np.argmin(cost)
    print(f"served {served:7.0e} tokens -> N={N[i] / 1e9:5.2f}B  D={D[i] / 1e9:7.0f}B  "
          f"D/N={D[i] / N[i]:6.0f}  total {cost[i]:.2e} FLOPs")
```

```text
target loss 2.1733 (7B params, 140B tokens)
served   0e+00 tokens -> N= 6.89B  D=    142B  D/N=    21  total 5.88e+21 FLOPs
served   1e+11 tokens -> N= 5.50B  D=    182B  D/N=    33  total 7.10e+21 FLOPs
served   1e+12 tokens -> N= 3.31B  D=    386B  D/N=   117  total 1.43e+22 FLOPs
served   1e+13 tokens -> N= 2.02B  D=   1342B  D/N=   665  total 5.66e+22 FLOPs
```

With no inference the answer is the Chinchilla point, 21 tokens per parameter. If the model will serve 10 trillion tokens, the cheapest way to the same quality is a model of 2B parameters on 1.3T tokens, 665 tokens per parameter, and each generated token then costs less than a third of what the 7B model would need. This is the economic logic behind 8B models trained on 15T tokens. It also explains why "over-trained" small models are good targets for [quantization and on-device inference](../11-llm-quantization/5-llm-small-models.mdx).

Over-training has limits. The data term $B / D^\beta$ shrinks slowly, so the extra tokens buy less and less, and at some point unique high-quality data runs out. Muennighoff et al. (2023) found that repeating data for up to about four epochs is almost as good as fresh data, with value falling to near zero after many more repetitions, and fitted a data-constrained version of the law with an effective data size.

## Other Scaling Questions

Scaling laws now cover much more than $N$ and $D$:

- **Hyperparameters.** DeepSeek LLM (2024) fitted power laws for the optimal batch size and learning rate as functions of compute, and measured model scale as non-embedding FLOPs per token instead of parameters. It also found that cleaner data shifts the optimum toward larger models, a reminder that the constants depend on the dataset. The [optimizers page](./3-llm-optimizers-and-schedules.mdx) covers $\mu$P, which tries to make the optimal learning rate independent of width.
- **Schedules.** MiniCPM (Hu et al., 2024) used a warmup-stable-decay schedule so that one long run gives losses for many token counts, and reported a much higher compute-optimal ratio of about 192 tokens per parameter for its setup. The ratio is not a law of nature; it depends on data, architecture and schedule.
- **Mixture of experts.** The relevant $N$ for compute is the active parameter count, while capacity grows with the total; the [mixture of experts section](../7-llm-mixture-of-experts/0-llm-moe-introduction.mdx) discusses this.
- **Downstream abilities.** Loss is smooth; benchmark scores can look sudden. Schaeffer et al. (2023, "Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?") argued that many "emergent" jumps come from discontinuous metrics such as exact match, and that smooth metrics on the same outputs scale smoothly.
- **Test-time compute.** Reasoning models scale a new axis: tokens spent thinking at inference time. See [reasoning models and test-time compute](../13-llm-advanced-architectures/3-llm-reasoning-models.mdx).

## Common Mistakes

- Treating "20 tokens per parameter" as the best way to train any model. It minimizes training compute for a given loss, not total cost. For a model that will be served heavily, train much longer.
- Mixing parameter definitions. Kaplan's laws use non-embedding parameters; Chinchilla's use all parameters. Embeddings are a large fraction of small models, so the choice changes the fitted exponent.
- Extrapolating a fit far beyond its data without error bars. A 0.05 error in an exponent becomes a several-fold error after five orders of magnitude.
- Comparing fitted constants across datasets or tokenizers. $E$, $A$ and $B$ are properties of one data distribution and one loss definition.
- Running scaling experiments with an untuned learning rate or a schedule length that does not match each run; this was one root of the Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreement.
- Forgetting that $C = 6ND$ ignores attention FLOPs, which matter at long context lengths.

## Summary

- Training compute is about $6ND$ FLOPs; scaling laws predict loss from $N$, $D$ and $C$.
- Kaplan et al. (2020) found clean power laws and recommended growing models faster than data ($N_\mathrm{opt} \propto C^{0.73}$).
- Chinchilla (2022) showed parameters and tokens should grow equally, giving about 20 tokens per parameter; a 70B model beat the 280B Gopher at the same budget.
- From $L = E + A/N^\alpha + B/D^\beta$ and $C = 6ND$ the optimum is $N_\mathrm{opt} = G (C/6)^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}$; the published constants were refitted in 2024 because they did not match the data.
- IsoFLOP profiles fit the optimum without a formula; Llama 3 used them to pick 405B parameters and 15.6T tokens.
- Inference cost pushes real models far past the Chinchilla point: hundreds to thousands of tokens per parameter.
